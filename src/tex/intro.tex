\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

The first detection of gravitational waves (GWs) from a binary black hole (BBH) merger was made by the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA (LVK) Collaboration on September 14, 2015. Since that fateful day, the LVK has detected nearly 100 compact binary coalescences (CBCs), bringing the third gravitational wave transient catalog (GWTC-3) up to 90 such events. \citep{2015CQGra..32g4001L,2015CQGra..32b4001A, 2016PhRvL.116f1102A,2019PhRvX...9c1040A,2021PhRvX..11b1053A,2021arXiv211103606T, 2021PTEP.2021eA102A}. With the maturation of GW Astronomy, novel studies of the universe are possible; we are now able to probe the low-redshift population of merging compact objects with much greater fidelity than with the sparse, early LVK catalogs \citep{2019ApJ...882L..24A,2021ApJ...913L...7A,2023PhRvX..13a1048A}. 

The source properties of CBCs are intrinsicly tied to the astrophysical processes that govern their formation, which can leave imprints on their population-level distributions \citep{10.3847/1538-4357/ab88b2, 1806.05820}. There are many proposed CBC formation pathways, though they broadly fall within two main categories: isolated binary evolution \citep{10.1017/S0074180900011839, 10.1086/340304, 1403.4754, 1701.02355} and dynamical assembly in dense star clusters \citep{10.1038/364423a0, 2000ApJ...528L..17P, 2013LRR....16....4B}. The former occurs in galatic fields and typically relies on some physical process(es) to shrink the initially wide orbital separation of the progenitor stars down to a distance small enough for the resulting compact objects to merge due to gravitational radiation. The latter relies on scattering and exchange interactions between astrophysical bodies in dense environments like globular clusters to form binaries capable of merging within a Hubble time. Component spins provide the clearest distinction between these two formation channels \citep{10.1088/1361-6382/aa552e, 10.1093/mnras/stx1764, 1609.05916, 2017Natur.548..426F,2018ApJ...854L...9F}, as the angle between component spins and the orbital angular momentum (referred to herein as the spin tilts) should be isotropically distributed for a population of dynamically formed BBHs, while physical processes and binary interactions tend to align component spins with the orbital angular momentum in isolated systems \citep{1981A&A....99..126H, 2005MNRAS.363...49K, 2013A&ARv..21...59I}, though some misalignment is possible via e.g. supernovea kicks \citep{10.1093/mnras/274.2.461}. Unfortunately, component spin degrees of freedom are poorly measured by GW data, making it difficult to identify the dominant formation channel in the observed catalog based on spin alone. 

On the other hand, component masses of individual events are typically inferred with higher precision than their spins, shining light on distinct features in the BBH mass distribution \citep{2021ApJ...913L..19T, 2023PhRvX..13a1048A, 2022ApJ...928..155T, 2022ApJ...924..101E,2022arXiv221012834E, 2208.11871, 2209.05766, 2301.00834}. Unfortunately, it is also challenging to distinguish between isolated and dynamical formation using only component mass, as both scenarios have been shown to produce mass distributions that significantly overlap (see the review from \citet{2022LRR....25....1M} for an extensive list of CBC population prediction studies). 

The predicted distributions of source properties of a given formation channel are typically estimated using Monte Carlo methods that approximate the stellar evolution processes leading to compact object formation (e.g., \texttt{startrack} \citep{2023arXiv230617602D}, \texttt{COMPAS} \citep{2022ApJS..258...34R}, \texttt{POSYDEN} \citep{10.3847/1538-4365/ac90c1, 2024arXiv241102376A}, \texttt{COSMIC} \citep{2021ascl.soft08022B}, \texttt{SEVN} \citep{2015MNRAS.451.4086S, 2017MNRAS.470.4739S}), and in some environments model the dynamics of such objects (e.g., \texttt{CMC} \citep{10.3847/1538-4365/ac2edf}, \texttt{CMC-COSMIC} \citep{2021ascl.soft08023R} \texttt{McFACTS} \citep{2024arXiv241016515M, 2024arXiv241110590C, 2024arXiv241018815D}, \texttt{cogsworth} \citep{2409.04543}). These predictions are sensitive to initial conditions and uncertainties in underlying physics, which has made it difficult to directly compare their predictions to population distributions and rates inferred by GW observations. Moreover, population models like those in \citet{2021arXiv211103606T} used to infer observed distributions and rates are typically simple parametrizations that do not account for correlations between source parameters. Incorporating correlations into a population anlaysis of BBH data could help to rule out certain areas of parameter space, potentially decreasing the overlap in predictions between different formation channels. 

Given the evidence for features, e.g. peaks or truncations, in the primary mass spectrum \citep{10.3847/2041-8213/aa9bf6, 10.3847/1538-4357/aab34c, 2019ApJ...882L..24A, 2021ApJ...913L...7A, 2023PhRvX..13a1048A,2104.07783v2, 2022ApJ...924..101E, 2022arXiv221012834E,2022ApJ...928..155T, 2301.00834,10.48550/arXiv.2302.07289}, in this work we attempt to identify a mass-spin correlation in the GWTC-3 catalog by allowing BBHs with similar primary masses to have spin distributions unique from the rest of the catalog. We then conduct a follow-up search for binaries elsewhere in the mass spectrum with similar spin properties. 

The remaining sections of this manuscript are structured as follows: Section \ref{sec:methods} describes the statistical framework and the different component models studied. Section \ref{sec:results} presents the results of our study, including the inferred branching ratios and the inferred subpopulation membership probabilities for each BBH in GWTC-3. In Section \ref{sec:astro} we discuss the implications of our findings and how it relates to the current understanding of compact binary formation and population synthesis. We finish in section \ref{sec:conclusion}, with a summary of our findings and prospects for building upon this work with future LVK catalogs.
